Intermittent Fasting Stages: What Happens in Your Body, Hour by Hour

Last updated: June 28, 2026 · by Clustle Studio

During a fast, your body moves through six overlapping metabolic stages — digestion, glucose depletion, ketosis onset, fat burning, deep ketosis, and autophagy. The approximate timings below are averages: the exact hour you reach each stage depends on your last meal, your activity, and how fat-adapted you are.

The six stages at a glance

Think of these as a gradual transition, not six switches that flip at exact times. They overlap, and the hour ranges shift from person to person.

StageApprox. timeWhat is happening
1. Digestion0–4 hYour body digests and absorbs your last meal. Blood sugar and insulin are elevated; the body runs mainly on glucose from the food.
2. Glucose depletion4–12 hBlood sugar settles and insulin falls. The body taps stored glycogen (in liver and muscle) for energy.
3. Ketosis onset12–16 hLiver glycogen runs low. The liver begins converting fat into ketones, an alternative fuel. This is where many people feel a shift.
4. Fat burning16–24 hFat oxidation rises and ketone levels climb. The body leans increasingly on stored fat for fuel.
5. Deep ketosis24–48 hKetone levels rise further and the body becomes more fully fat-adapted for the duration of the fast.
6. Autophagy~24 h+A cellular "clean-up" process in which cells recycle damaged components. Believed to ramp up after roughly a day of fasting and more during prolonged fasts (see the note below).

These ranges are widely cited averages for healthy adults. They are estimates, not medical thresholds, and individuals vary considerably.

Stage by stage, explained

1. Digestion (0–4 hours)

Right after eating, your body is busy absorbing nutrients. Insulin is high to move glucose into cells, and any energy surplus is stored. You are not yet "fasting" in a metabolic sense — you are running on the meal you just ate.

2. Glucose depletion (4–12 hours)

As the meal is fully processed, blood sugar and insulin drop back to baseline. To keep energy steady, the body draws on glycogen — glucose stored in the liver and muscles. Toward the end of this window, liver glycogen starts to run low.

3. Ketosis onset (around 12–16 hours)

When liver glycogen is depleted, the liver begins breaking down fatty acids into ketones, which the brain and body can use for fuel. This is the metabolic shift most associated with intermittent fasting. The 16:8 schedule (16 hours fasting) is popular partly because it brings many people to the edge of this stage.

4. Fat burning (16–24 hours)

With ketone production established, the body relies more heavily on stored fat. Fat oxidation does not switch on suddenly — it rises gradually as glycogen falls — but by this window it is clearly the dominant fuel source for many people.

5. Deep ketosis (24–48 hours)

During longer fasts, ketone levels continue to rise and the body becomes more fully fat-adapted. This stage is generally reached only during extended fasting, not on a typical daily 16:8 routine.

6. Autophagy (roughly 24 hours and beyond)

Autophagy is a process in which cells break down and recycle damaged parts — a kind of cellular housekeeping. It is one of the most discussed benefits of fasting, but also the least precisely measured in humans.

An honest note on autophagy timing: Much of what is known about fasting-induced autophagy comes from animal and cell studies. The exact point at which it meaningfully increases in humans — and the popular "24-hour" or "48-hour" figures — are estimates, not settled facts. Treat any specific number as a rough guide.

What changes when you reach each stage

The hour ranges above are averages. Your personal timing shifts with:

  • Your last meal — a large, carb-heavy meal extends the glucose and glycogen stages; a smaller or lower-carb meal shortens them.
  • Activity — exercise burns through glycogen faster, which can bring ketosis forward.
  • Fat-adaptation — people who fast regularly or eat lower-carb often reach ketosis sooner.
  • Sleep, stress, and muscle mass — all nudge the timing in smaller ways.

This is why two people on the same 16-hour fast can be in slightly different stages at hour 14.

Frequently asked questions

What are the stages of intermittent fasting?

Six overlapping stages: digestion (0–4 h), glucose depletion (4–12 h), ketosis onset (~12–16 h), fat burning (16–24 h), deep ketosis (24–48 h), and autophagy (ramping up after roughly 24 h). The ranges are approximate and vary by person.

When does ketosis start during a fast?

For most people, around 12–16 hours in, once liver glycogen runs low. Exact timing depends on your last meal, activity, and how carb-adapted you are.

When does autophagy begin during fasting?

It is thought to increase after roughly 24 hours and to become more pronounced during longer fasts, but the precise timing in humans is not firmly established.

How long until your body starts burning fat?

The shift toward fat as the main fuel generally happens between 16 and 24 hours, though fat burning rises gradually well before that rather than switching on at once.

Does everyone enter these stages at the same time?

No. Timing varies with meal size and composition, activity, sleep, muscle mass, and fat-adaptation. The hours are averages, not exact thresholds.
