OMAD (One Meal a Day): A Practical Beginner's Guide

Last updated: July 9, 2026 · by Clustle Studio

OMAD — One Meal A Day — means eating all of your food in a single window, usually about an hour, and fasting the rest of the time. That works out to roughly a 23:1 schedule. It is one of the most demanding fasting patterns, and this guide treats it honestly: strong on simplicity and calorie control, but genuinely hard to do well. Consider it an advanced option, not a starting line.

What OMAD actually is

Intermittent fasting patterns are usually described by their fasting-to-eating ratio. A common beginner pattern is 16:8 — sixteen hours fasting, an eight-hour eating window. OMAD pushes that much further: one meal, one short window, and roughly 23 hours of fasting. You can see how it compares with gentler schedules in our overview of intermittent fasting schedules.

In practice, "one hour" is a guideline rather than a rule. Most people who do OMAD eat one substantial meal and finish within about 60 minutes. The core idea is the same either way: a single, large, deliberately built meal instead of food spread across the day.

How OMAD works metabolically

OMAD works through two overlapping mechanisms. The first is simply calorie control: for many people, having one eating window makes it harder to over-eat across the day, which can create the calorie deficit behind weight change. The second is the length of the fast itself. A ~23-hour fast keeps insulin low for an extended stretch and moves the body further along the metabolic timeline — glycogen runs down, fat oxidation rises, and many people spend a meaningful part of the day in ketosis. Our hour-by-hour guide to fasting stages walks through that progression in detail.

It is worth being careful here: much of the specific timing and the more dramatic claims about long fasts are approximate, and the human evidence is mixed. OMAD is not magic. Its main, reasonably well-supported effect is that it makes eating less over the day easier for some people — not everyone.

The honest pros and cons

OMAD's strengths and weaknesses are two sides of the same coin. The single window that makes it simple is the same window that makes nutrition hard.

Realistic prosRealistic cons
Simplicity. One meal to plan, shop for, and clean up. No tracking breakfast and lunch.Nutrition is hard. Fitting a full day of vitamins, minerals, fibre and especially protein into one sitting is genuinely difficult.
Strong calorie control. A single window makes accidental over-eating less likely for many people.Hunger. A ~23-hour fast can bring real hunger, low energy, irritability, or trouble concentrating, particularly at first.

These trade-offs are general. How they land for you depends on your body, schedule, and goals — and some people simply do not get on with OMAD, which is fine.

How to do OMAD more safely

If you have decided to try OMAD, a few habits make it more sustainable and lower the risk of under-eating — the most common problem with this schedule.

  • Build the meal around protein. Because you only eat once, hitting your protein target is the hardest part. Anchor the plate with a solid protein source, then add vegetables, whole carbs, and healthy fats.
  • Do not under-eat. OMAD makes it easy to accidentally eat too little for the day. The goal is one big, balanced meal — not a small one.
  • Hydrate through the fast. Water, plain black coffee and unsweetened tea are the usual choices. Some people add electrolytes or a little salt to ease headaches and light-headedness.
  • Ease in. Work up from a shorter window rather than jumping straight to 23 hours. Starting with 16:8 first is the common path.
  • Keep some resistance training. Combined with enough protein, it is one of the main levers people use to protect muscle while fasting.
  • Watch how you feel. Persistent dizziness, poor sleep, mood changes, or disordered thoughts about food are signals to stop and reassess — see our notes on common fasting side effects.

When you do eat, it also helps to end the fast sensibly — a huge, rushed meal on an empty stomach can feel awful. Our guide on how to break a fast covers this in more depth.

Who OMAD suits — and who should not try it

OMAD tends to suit people who already fast comfortably at shorter windows, who value simplicity, who can build one large, nutritionally complete meal, and whose schedule and social life can accommodate skipping other meals. It is best framed as an advanced schedule you graduate to, not one you begin with.

It is not appropriate for a number of groups, and this matters more than any potential benefit:

Frequently asked questions

What is OMAD?

OMAD stands for One Meal A Day. You eat all of your day's food within a single window — usually about one hour — and fast for the rest, which works out to roughly a 23:1 schedule. It is one of the more demanding intermittent fasting patterns and is best treated as an advanced option rather than a starting point.

Is OMAD good for beginners?

For most beginners, no. OMAD is an aggressive schedule that makes hunger, nutrition, and protein targets harder to manage than gentler patterns. Many people do better starting with 16:8 or a similar approach and only moving toward OMAD later, if at all. There is no requirement to fast this long to benefit from intermittent fasting.

How many calories should you eat on OMAD?

It depends on your body, goals, and activity level, and it is worth confirming with a professional. The practical challenge is the opposite of most diets: because you have only one meal, it can be hard to eat enough — especially enough protein and micronutrients — rather than too much. Deliberately building a large, balanced meal matters more than counting to a precise number.

What can you drink during the OMAD fasting window?

Water is the mainstay, and staying hydrated is important during a long fast. Plain black coffee and unsweetened tea are widely considered fine for most people. Some people add a pinch of salt or electrolytes to water, which may help with the light-headedness or headaches that can come from long fasts. Anything with meaningful calories will break the fast.

Who should not try OMAD?

OMAD is not appropriate for people who are pregnant or breastfeeding, anyone with a current or past eating disorder, people with diabetes or who take blood-sugar-lowering medication, those who are underweight, teenagers, or people with other medical conditions — unless a qualified clinician has specifically advised it. If any of these apply to you, speak to a healthcare professional before fasting this long.

Will OMAD slow your metabolism or cause muscle loss?

The evidence is mixed and depends heavily on how you do it. Eating enough total food and enough protein in your one meal, plus doing some resistance training, are the main levers people use to protect muscle. Chronically under-eating on OMAD is a real risk, and that — not the fasting window itself — is what tends to cause problems.
