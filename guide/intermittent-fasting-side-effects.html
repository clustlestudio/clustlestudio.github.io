Intermittent Fasting Side Effects (and How to Manage Them)

Last updated: July 9, 2026 · by Clustle Studio

Most people who try intermittent fasting run into at least one uncomfortable side effect — hunger, headaches, low energy, irritability, poor sleep, dizziness, constipation, or feeling cold. For many people these are mild and settle within the first week or two as the body adapts. This guide explains why each happens and what tends to help, and — importantly — who should avoid fasting altogether.

The common side effects at a glance

These are the effects most people report, especially early on. None of them are guaranteed to happen to you, and their intensity varies widely from person to person.

Each side effect, explained

Hunger

Hunger is the most predictable side effect, and also one of the most misunderstood. It tends to come in waves tied to your usual meal times rather than building endlessly, and for many people a wave passes within twenty to thirty minutes. Water, black coffee, or unsweetened tea can take the edge off. As your body adapts to a routine, the hunger signals at old meal times often quieten. If you are just getting started, our guide on how to start 16:8 intermittent fasting covers easing in so hunger never gets overwhelming.

Headaches

Fasting headaches usually trace back to three things: dehydration, dropping blood sugar, and caffeine withdrawal if you normally drink coffee and skip it. The most reliable fixes are drinking enough water, adding a small amount of salt or an electrolyte drink, and not quitting caffeine at the exact moment you start fasting. Layering too many changes at once is a common reason the first week feels harder than it needs to.

Low energy and fatigue

In the early days your body is adjusting to longer gaps between meals and to leaning more on stored fuel. It is common to feel a dip, particularly in the afternoon. Easing in — shortening your eating window gradually rather than jumping to long fasts — helps, as does prioritising sleep and keeping workouts lighter on longer fasting days. For many people energy stabilises after the first week or two, though this is not universal.

Irritability ("hanger")

Low blood sugar and hunger can genuinely affect mood, focus, and patience — the informal term is "hanger." If you know you have demanding or people-facing hours, it can help to position your fasting window so the hungriest stretch lands during quieter time. Making sure your eating window actually delivers enough food and nutrients matters too; under-eating overall is a frequent hidden cause.

Poor sleep

Fasting can cut both ways for sleep. Some people sleep better; others find hunger, or an eating window that runs too late, disrupts it. Finishing eating earlier in the evening and avoiding late caffeine are the usual levers. What breaks a fast — and whether that late-night drink counts — is covered in our guide on what breaks a fast.

Dizziness and lightheadedness

Shifts in blood sugar, fluid, and salt can leave you lightheaded, especially standing up quickly. Hydration and electrolytes (sodium, potassium, magnesium) help many people, along with simply moving more slowly. Mild, occasional lightheadedness that resolves is one thing; dizziness that leads to fainting, or comes with a racing heart or confusion, is a signal to stop, sit or lie down, and get medical advice.

Constipation

Eat less and drink less, and there is simply less moving through your digestive system. Staying hydrated, getting enough fibre and vegetables during your eating window, and staying physically active all help keep things regular.

Feeling cold

Digesting food generates heat, so going without it — combined with lower fluid volume — can leave you feeling chilly, particularly in the hands and feet. Warm drinks, an extra layer, and staying hydrated usually settle it. Feeling persistently, uncomfortably cold can also be a sign you are over-extending your fasts or under-eating overall, which is worth taking seriously.

Practical ways to reduce side effects

No single trick works for everyone, but a handful of habits reliably take the edge off for most people:

  • Hydrate. Many "fasting" symptoms — headaches, dizziness, fatigue — are partly dehydration. Water is the single most useful tool.
  • Mind your electrolytes. Sodium, potassium and magnesium matter when you are eating less. A pinch of salt or an electrolyte drink helps many people (see the note below on medication).
  • Ease in. Start with a shorter fasting window and lengthen it over weeks rather than days. This is the biggest lever for a gentler adjustment.
  • Time it around your life. Put the hungriest hours during quieter parts of your day, and finish eating earlier if sleep suffers.
  • Eat well in your window. Fasting is not a licence to under-eat. Enough protein, fibre and overall nutrition reduces fatigue, hanger and constipation.

Who should avoid intermittent fasting

This is the part that matters most. Fasting is genuinely not appropriate for some people, and for others it should only be done under medical supervision. You should not start intermittent fasting without talking to a healthcare professional first if any of the following apply:

  • Pregnant or breastfeeding — nutritional needs are higher and restriction can be harmful.
  • A history of eating disorders — structured food restriction can trigger or worsen disordered eating.
  • Diabetes or medication affecting blood sugar or blood pressure — fasting can cause dangerous lows and doses may need adjusting; this requires medical guidance.
  • Underweight, or children and teenagers — growth and adequate intake take priority over fasting.
  • Other chronic medical conditions — including some heart, kidney and liver conditions; check first.

Even if none of these apply, side effects that are severe, worsening, or include fainting, chest pain, or confusion are not something to push through. Stop and seek advice.

Frequently asked questions

Are intermittent fasting side effects normal?

Mild side effects such as hunger, headaches, low energy and irritability are common in the first days to weeks as your body adjusts, and for many people they fade once a routine settles. Symptoms that are severe, persistent, or include fainting, chest pain or confusion are not something to push through — stop and speak with a healthcare professional.

Why do I get headaches when fasting?

Fasting headaches are often linked to dehydration, dropping blood sugar, and — for regular coffee drinkers — caffeine withdrawal. Drinking water, not skipping your usual coffee entirely at first, and adding a little salt or electrolytes helps many people. Easing into fasting gradually rather than jumping straight to long fasts also tends to reduce them.

How long do intermittent fasting side effects last?

For most people the roughest patch is the first one to two weeks while the body adapts to going longer between meals. Timing varies a lot from person to person. If side effects are still significant after several weeks, or they get worse rather than better, that is a signal to reassess your approach or check in with a healthcare professional.

Can I drink water and electrolytes while fasting?

Yes. Plain water, and for many people black coffee or unsweetened tea, are fine during a fast, and staying hydrated is one of the most effective ways to reduce headaches, dizziness and fatigue. Sodium, potassium and magnesium from electrolytes can help too, though anyone on blood pressure or other medication should check with their doctor before adding electrolyte supplements.

Who should not try intermittent fasting?

Fasting is not appropriate for everyone. People who are pregnant or breastfeeding, anyone with a history of eating disorders, people who are underweight, children and teenagers, and people with diabetes or on medication that affects blood sugar or blood pressure should not fast without medical supervision. If any of these apply to you, talk to a qualified healthcare professional first.

Does feeling cold or dizzy while fasting mean something is wrong?

Feeling slightly cold or lightheaded when standing up can happen as blood sugar and fluid levels shift, and is often eased by hydration, electrolytes and moving more slowly. But dizziness that leads to fainting, or that comes with a racing heart or confusion, is not something to ignore — sit or lie down, break your fast if needed, and seek medical advice if it recurs.
