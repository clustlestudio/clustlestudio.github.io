The 5:2 Diet: How It Works, Pros, Cons and How to Start

Last updated: July 9, 2026 · by Clustle Studio

The 5:2 diet is a form of intermittent fasting where you eat normally for five days a week and cut down to roughly 500–600 calories on two non-consecutive days. It appeals to people who dislike restricting food every single day — but the two low-calorie days can be genuinely tough. This guide covers how it works, how it compares to daily methods like 16:8, and how to start without overdoing it.

What the 5:2 diet actually is

On the 5:2 plan, most of the week looks completely ordinary: you eat the way you normally would on five days and don't count anything. On the remaining two days — often called "fast days," though you still eat — you keep intake to around 500 calories for women and 600 for men. Those numbers are widely quoted guidelines, not exact medical prescriptions, and the two days are usually spaced apart (for example Monday and Thursday) rather than back to back.

The idea, popularised as the "Fast Diet," is that a short, contained period of low intake is easier to stick with than permanent daily dieting. Because five days a week require no rules, many people find it simpler to fit around real life than a plan that governs every meal.

How 5:2 differs from time-restricted eating

It's easy to lump every fasting method together, but 5:2 and daily time-restricted eating work along different axes. One controls how much you eat on a couple of days; the other controls when you eat every day.

The realistic pros

  • Flexibility. Five days a week involve no counting, tracking, or off-limits foods. This freedom is the plan's biggest selling point.
  • No daily fasting. Unlike time-restricted eating, you're not managing hunger or a clock every single day — just twice a week.
  • Simple to grasp. "Two low days, five normal days" is easy to remember and explain, with no elaborate meal plan required.
  • May lower weekly calories. Two sharply reduced days can bring down total weekly intake — provided you don't compensate by overeating on the other five.

The honest cons

  • Fast days can be hard. Five to six hundred calories is very little. Hunger, irritability, low energy, and trouble concentrating are common, especially in the first few weeks.
  • It takes planning. Hitting a low target without feeling miserable means thinking ahead about filling, low-calorie meals rather than improvising.
  • Easy to over-compensate. Some people eat more than usual on normal days to "make up" for fast days, which can cancel out any calorie deficit.
  • Social friction. A fast day landing on a dinner invitation or work lunch is awkward, though the non-consecutive schedule gives some room to shuffle.
  • Not for everyone. The steep drop on fast days makes it a poor fit for several groups (see the safety note below).

Who it tends to suit

The 5:2 diet often works best for people who dislike daily restriction but can tolerate two demanding days a week, and who have a fairly regular routine that lets them place fast days on predictably quiet days. It also appeals to people who find calorie-counting every day exhausting and prefer to concentrate the effort into short bursts.

It tends to suit people less well if they have very physically demanding jobs, train hard most days, get intense hunger that affects mood or focus, or find that a "restrict then normal" pattern nudges them toward bingeing. If any of that sounds familiar, a gentler daily approach — or comparing fasting against plain calorie counting — may be a better path.

How to start safely

  1. Check it's appropriate for you. Read the safety note at the end and, if in doubt, talk to a healthcare professional first.
  2. Pick two non-consecutive days. Choose days that are typically calm and low on social meals — many people use something like Monday and Thursday.
  3. Plan your fast-day food in advance. Build meals around protein and fibre (eggs, vegetables, soup, lean fish or chicken, plain yoghurt) that are filling for few calories. Decide whether you'll spread the calories out or eat one or two small meals.
  4. Stay hydrated. Water, black coffee, and unsweetened tea are calorie-free and genuinely help take the edge off hunger.
  5. Eat normally — not excessively — on the other five days. "Normal" means your usual balanced eating, not a reward for the fast days.
  6. Ease in. Some people start with one low-calorie day a week before moving to two, giving their appetite time to adjust.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Bingeing after a fast day. Treating the next day as a free-for-all is the quickest way to undo the plan.
  • Putting fast days back to back. Two low days in a row is much harder and unnecessary — the point of non-consecutive days is recovery in between.
  • Filling the budget with empty calories. Spending 500 calories on refined snacks leaves you hungrier than spending them on protein and vegetables.
  • Ignoring how you feel. Persistent dizziness, weakness, or low mood are signals to stop and reassess, not to push through.
  • Expecting a guaranteed outcome. Results vary widely from person to person; the pattern is a tool, not a promise.

Frequently asked questions

What is the 5:2 diet?

It's a form of intermittent fasting where you eat normally on five days a week and cut back to roughly 500–600 calories on the other two. The two low-calorie days are usually kept non-consecutive, for example Monday and Thursday. It's sometimes called the Fast Diet.

How many calories do you eat on 5:2 fasting days?

The commonly cited targets are about 500 calories for women and 600 for men on each of the two fasting days. These are rough guidelines rather than precise prescriptions, and some people spread the calories across the day while others eat them in one or two meals.

How is 5:2 different from 16:8?

5:2 limits calories on two whole days a week but places no restriction on when you eat the other five. The 16:8 method restricts eating to an 8-hour daily window every day but sets no specific calorie cap. In short, 5:2 controls how much on a couple of days; 16:8 controls when you eat each day.

Who should not try the 5:2 diet?

It's not suitable for everyone. People who are pregnant or breastfeeding, those with a history of eating disorders, people with diabetes or on blood-sugar-lowering medication, children and teenagers, and anyone who is underweight should avoid it or only try it under medical supervision. Speak to a qualified healthcare professional before starting.

Will the 5:2 diet help me lose weight?

It can help some people because cutting to 500–600 calories on two days usually lowers total weekly intake — but only if you don't overeat on the other five days. Results vary widely, and no eating pattern guarantees weight loss. It appears to work mainly by reducing overall calories, not through anything unique to fasting itself.

What can you eat and drink on a 5:2 fasting day?

Most people focus on high-protein, high-fibre foods that are filling for few calories — eggs, vegetables, soup, lean fish or chicken, plain yoghurt. Water, black coffee, and unsweetened tea are calorie-free and help manage hunger. There's no required menu; the aim is simply to stay near the calorie target.
