Intermittent Fasting Schedules Compared: 16:8, 18:6, 20:4, OMAD & 5:2

Last updated: July 6, 2026 · by Clustle Studio

The number in a fasting schedule is just fasting hours : eating hours. 16:8 means a 16-hour fast and an 8-hour window to eat. The honest truth up front: the "best" schedule isn't the most extreme one — it's the one you can actually keep doing. Here's how the main options compare.

These are general patterns for healthy adults, not prescriptions. Any of them only works if it fits your life.

16:8 — the default starting point

A 16-hour fast and an 8-hour eating window, usually done by skipping breakfast and eating from, say, noon to 8pm. It's popular because it's simple, flexible, and easy to sustain. For most people starting out, this is the one to try. If 16 hours feels hard at first, begin with 14:10 and tighten up over two weeks. See our full 16:8 starter guide.

18:6 — a modest step up

An 18-hour fast with a 6-hour window (e.g. 1pm–7pm). It's a natural next step once 16:8 feels routine, giving a slightly longer time in fat-burning stages and a somewhat tighter eating window, without the difficulty of the more extreme schedules.

20:4 — the "Warrior Diet"

Twenty hours fasting, a 4-hour window, typically one large meal plus a snack. It can produce a bigger calorie deficit, but the short window makes it harder to get enough protein and nutrients, and easier to overeat. Best for people who already fast comfortably and want more.

OMAD — one meal a day

Roughly 23:1: a single meal in a 1–2 hour window. Its appeal is total simplicity — you plan one meal and you're done. The trade-offs are real: hitting your full nutrition needs in one sitting is hard, it can feel socially restrictive, and it's easy to under-eat or binge. OMAD is something to work up to, not start with.

5:2 — the weekly approach

Instead of a daily window, you eat normally five days a week and cap two non-consecutive days at about 500–600 calories. It suits people who'd rather not watch the clock every day. The downside is that the two low-calorie days can be genuinely tough, and some people overeat on normal days.

So which should you pick?

  • New to fasting? Start with 16:8 (or 14:10 for a week first).
  • 16:8 feels easy and you want more? Move to 18:6.
  • Hate tracking every day? Try 5:2.
  • Want maximum simplicity and you're experienced? 20:4 or OMAD.

Whatever you pick, consistency beats intensity. A schedule you follow 6 days a week will always beat a stricter one you quit after a week. A tracker that shows your window and streak — like StreakFast — makes it much easier to stay consistent.

A note on health: This is general information, not medical advice. If you're pregnant, breastfeeding, have diabetes or a history of disordered eating, or take medication, talk to a doctor before starting any fasting schedule — and be cautious with the longer ones (20:4, OMAD).

Frequently asked questions

Which intermittent fasting schedule is best for beginners?

16:8 — a 16-hour fast and 8-hour window — is the easiest to sustain. Many people ease in with 14:10 first.

What's the difference between 16:8 and OMAD?

16:8 gives an 8-hour window for two or three meals; OMAD is one meal in a 1–2 hour window (~23:1). OMAD is much stricter and best worked up to.

Is 5:2 effective for weight loss?

Yes, for many people, because it lowers weekly calories. It suits those who prefer not to restrict daily, though the two low-calorie days can be hard.

Which schedule gives the fastest results?

Longer fasts (20:4, OMAD) create a bigger deficit for many and can show faster scale results, but they're harder to sustain. The one you keep doing wins.
